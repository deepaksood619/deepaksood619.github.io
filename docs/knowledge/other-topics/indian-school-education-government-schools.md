Indian government schools serve about half of all schoolchildren, but learning outcomes, infrastructure and funding lag badly. The policy exists on paper (RTE Act, NEP 2020, Samagra Shiksha); the gap is in implementation and funding. Figures below are from a Hindi explainer (Jist, Aug 2026) citing UDISE+, ASER, PRS and NITI Aayog; some numbers come from auto-captions and are uncertain.

## Key Points

- **Scale (UDISE+ 2024-25):** about 24.69 crore students, 14.72 lakh schools and over 1 crore teachers; about 69% of schools are government-run
- **Learning (ASER):** only about 45% of class 5 government-school children can read a class 2 text, and only about a quarter can do basic division; about 43 lakh secondary dropouts in 2024-25
- **Closures (UDISE+ 2025-26):** 877 government schools closed in a year (about 22 a day) while 2,106 private schools opened; government schools lost about 86 lakh students over 2023-24 to 2025-26 while private schools gained about 88 lakh; NITI Aayog counts about 94,000 government school closures in 10 years
- **Cost:** a government school costs about ₹2,863 per child per year against about ₹25,000 for a private school (roughly 9x), so private fees are a large share of income for low and middle-income families; about 19.6 crore people live in households under ₹1.25 lakh a year and about 73.2 crore in the ₹1.25-5 lakh band (PRICE ICE 360)
- **Mid-day meal (PM POSHAN):** ₹6.78 per day for classes 1-5 and ₹10.17 per day for classes 6-8

## Funding Gap

- The Kothari Commission (1964-66) recommended spending 6% of GDP on education, repeated in the 1968 and 1986 policies and in NEP 2020
- Actual spending in 2022-23 (Centre plus states, PRS analysis) was about 4.1% of GDP, and that covers higher education, teacher education and scholarships too
- Most school money goes to salaries, then pensions, leaving little for infrastructure
- Economist Jandhyala Tilak argues 6% is feasible with political will; Jean Dreze describes "islands of excellence in a sea of deprivation", meaning model schools starve the wider network; Reetika Khera asks whether the money reaches the child; CAG audits repeatedly flag teacher deployment, training, infrastructure and monitoring gaps

## Comparison

- **Finland:** spends about 6.4% of GDP (Sweden about 7.3%); basic education is fee-free including books, meals, health care and transport; teachers need a Master's degree and have curriculum autonomy; the private school share is near zero
- **Singapore (2024):** 336 schools, of which 244 government, 76 government-aided and 8 independent; the children of policymakers and teachers attend them
- **Core argument:** in India officials, politicians and even many government teachers do not send their own children to these schools, so the people who could fix them have no stake in them

## Context (July-August 2026)

- After the NEET paper-leak protest at Jantar Mantar (6 Jun-25 Jul 2026), school children in classes 6-10 in 10+ states protested over basic facilities, starting on 28 Jul 2026 in Fatehpur, UP (about 1,000 students). Demands: drinking water, working fans, clean toilets, roof repairs and teachers
- Worm-infested mid-day meals were reported in several districts, and some Rajasthan and UP districts banned outsiders filming in government schools; the video's critique is that hiding the camera does not fix the problem
- The problems are old; media attention (salience bias) surged only after one event
