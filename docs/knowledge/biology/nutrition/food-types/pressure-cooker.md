Pressure cookers are not used as universally in Western countries as they are in India because of different cooking styles, kitchen appliances, and historical safety fears.

## Why It's Not Popular Outside India

- **Boiling vs. roasting** — Indian cooking relies heavily on boiling, stewing, and making gravies (dal, curries, rice), which fit a pressure cooker perfectly
- **Western techniques** — Western and many other global cuisines focus more on baking, roasting, frying, and grilling, where a pressure cooker isn't useful
- **Ovens and slow cookers** — Western kitchens are often built around ovens, stovetop pans, and slow cookers, used instead of pressure cookers to roast meats or simmer stews over long hours
- **Fear of explosions** — older generations in Western countries remember stories or accidents involving old stovetop pressure cookers blowing their lids off
- **Loss of control** — many home cooks prefer pots where they can easily see and stir the food while it cooks, rather than sealing it under high pressure

Note: traditional stovetop "whistling" pressure cookers are rare in places like the US or UK, but multi-cookers like the Instant Pot have recently made pressure cooking popular again there.

## Traditional Stovetop vs. Electric Multi-Cooker

- **The "whistle" count** — Indian stovetop cookers rely on a weight valve that vents pressure by lifting and whistling; Indian recipes are usually written by counting whistles (e.g. "cook for 3 whistles"). Electric multi-cookers are silent and cook based strictly on digital timers
- **Heat control** — stovetop cookers require manually adjusting the flame from high to low; electric cookers adjust their own internal temperature automatically using built-in sensors
- **Safety features** — traditional cookers have a simple safety valve that melts or pops if pressure gets too high; electric multi-cookers feature over a dozen digital safety mechanisms, including lid-locking pins and auto-shutoff sensors, making them nearly impossible to explode

| Feature | Traditional Indian Stovetop Cooker | Modern Electric Multi-Cooker |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanics | Manual flame adjustment | Automated digital controls |
| Monitoring | Requires counting audible whistles | Requires setting a digital timer |
| Materials | Primarily aluminium or stainless steel | Heavy stainless steel or non-stick inner pots |
| Versatility | Only pressure cooks and sautés | Pressure cooks, slow cooks, makes yogurt, steams, and bakes |
| Portability | Requires a stove burner | Plug-and-play anywhere with a power outlet |
| Speed | Heats up and builds pressure faster | Takes longer to heat up and build pressure |

## Why Stovetop Cookers Still Dominate in India

- **Speed and power** — Indian stovetop cookers heat up much faster on high gas flames than electric elements
- **Habit** — Indian home cooks are highly accustomed to cooking by the sound of whistles
- **Cost** — traditional aluminium or steel cookers are significantly cheaper and last for decades without needing electronic repairs

## Instant Pot Basics

- **What it is** — an electric pressure cooker, not a slow cooker: food cooks only by steam and liquid, so nothing crisps or bakes, and it is never used to pressure fry. The slow-cook function is mediocre
- **Sizes** — 6-qt is the standard (most recipes are tested on it); 8-qt for big families; 3-qt mini for sides, yogurt and single servings; 10-qt is not recommended for beginners because recipes need liquid and time adjustments
- **Parts** — heating element in the base (never put food between base and liner), stainless inner pot, silicone sealing ring (must be snug or no pressure builds; store the lid upside down to avoid odours), sealing/venting knob, float pin, and a condensation collector at the back (clean it to avoid mould)
- **Controls** — Manual is the same as Pressure Cook, so the other presets can be ignored in favour of manual plus a recipe time; default is high pressure (almost every recipe uses high); time ranges from 0 min (tender veg like broccoli) to 240 min
- **Saute** — lid must be off (lid on auto-cancels); use the More setting; maximum 30 min per session, then cancel and restart; the display shows "Hot" once preheated
- **Minimum liquid** — 1 cup (6-qt), 1.5-2 cups (8-qt), 0.5 cup (3-qt); there is almost no evaporation, so dishes come out watery, so reduce them with saute afterwards
- **Water test** — run a new pot once before cooking: 1-2 cups water, knob on Sealing, manual 5 min on high
- **Release** — natural release (NPR) lets pressure fall on its own; quick release turns the knob to Venting (loud, warn people); the float pin dropping means it is safe to open. Never move, unplug or carry a pressurised pot, and never cover it with a towel while it depressurises
- **Indian cooking examples** — dal on the multigrain preset takes 15 min at high pressure plus about 5 min natural release; rice takes about 17-18 min hands-free; ghee from butter in saute takes about 10-12 min unattended; tadka can be done in the same pot

**Indian recipes from a 3-year owner review** ([My Instant Pot review, Hindi](https://youtu.be/IY9QUzZMRoY)):

- **Rice:** jeera rice by sauteing oil and cumin, adding washed basmati at 1-1.5 cups water per cup of rice (1:1 for broken rice), then pressure cooking on low for about 10 minutes (high pressure breaks the grains)
- **Beans and dal:** cooks on high pressure with a reduced time (15 min on low is about 10 or 8 min on high); rajma is soaked overnight, with the masala sauteed in the same pot, then 15-16 min on high. Electric cookers retain water, so use much less than in a stovetop cooker
- **Yogurt:** heat milk on warm mode and saute for the last ~5 minutes to boil it without scorching, cool it to just warm, add the culture, and use yogurt mode (8 hours by default)
- **Idli:** steam mode with a little water and the knob on Venting (steam only, no pressure), about 10 minutes once steam rises
- **Care:** wash the lid and sealing ring with soap after each use or onion and pasta smells carry into the next dish, never put a hand over the vent, and never add water without the inner pot. The one drawback is dependence on electricity where voltage is unstable

## Buying an Electric Multi-Cooker

Five criteria from a reviewer who tested nine models since 2021 (only two met all five; the captions leave unclear which, most likely the Instant Pots):

- **Intuitive control panel** — a clear Start button and working presets for daily foods (beans, rice); judge it by watching someone use it
- **Clear progress indicator** — avoid screens that only say "heat"; good ones show stage lights, a progress bar with a "safe to open" message, or pressure percentage with release guidance
- **Hands-free steam release** — a switch or button away from the valve, not a utensil on the valve; the lid should also direct steam away from the counter
- **Anti-spin inner pot** — the pot must not rotate when stirring, essential if you saute or sear in it
- **Non-toxic inner pot** — stainless steel or certified PFAS-free ceramic coating; avoid nonstick (PFAS) given the heat and pressure

## Kitchen Appliances for Indian Cooking

A homemaker's five everyday appliances: Instant Pot, electric mini chopper, air fryer, stand mixer and roti maker.

- **Air fryer** — a borosilicate glass base (dishwasher and microwave safe, doubles as storage) avoids cheap nonstick coatings; used oil-free for veg tikkis, roasted brinjal for baingan bharta (15 min) and paneer tikka
- **Mini chopper** — a glass base avoids contact with microplastics; used for a week's minced garlic and soy chunks
- **Stand mixer** — dough hook for roti, bread and pizza dough
- **Roti maker** — needs a slightly softer dough; rotis come out about 75-80% as good as on a gas stove and turn chewy when cool. On an induction hob, a roti jali only works on hot plates, not induction
