By Greg McKeown

- How to do better by doing less
- We should be focusing on what we should do, thinking instead about what is essential to our happiness and well-being
- 4 main points
    - Do less, but do it better
    - Reject the notion that we should accomplish everything
    - Constantly question yourself and update plans accordingly
    - Changes are put in place
- **Learned helplessness,** that is, becoming so used to the feeling of being overwhelmed that we approach our lives with passivity. (Dogs with electric shock and a lever with no effect choose to stay in shock zone, because they have learned helplessness)
    - Opposite - [learned industriousness](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2020/11/02/motivation/#07-05)
- Take a few hours of blank time for thinking about yourself and always see the bigger picture on why you are doing what you do
- Sleep and play are as important as work
- Be ruthless in cutting away things that aren't essential
- Say "no" to nonessential tasks and plan the essential ones carefully (How will I know when I have reached my goal)
- Stop doing unncessary things by withdrawing from failure and setting boundaries (Admit errors and mistakes and let go)
- Keeping on top of what's important requires that you eliminate what slows you down and elminate it.
- Always take 50% of buffer time that you have expected a work to take
- Building upon previous progress with small, incremental steps. Small wins create momentum, which gives you the confidence to further succeed.
- Routine is very important to achieve long term goals

## Practical Habits

From [Essentialism by Greg McKeown (Productivity Game)](https://youtu.be/uDL1b5m-cYI). McKeown's origin story: he went to a client meeting on the Friday his daughter was born, to please a colleague. Saying yes to please others means losing sight of what is essential.

- **Three rights:** pick commitments by the right thing, the right reason and the right time
- **Weekly half-hour reset** (Sunday afternoon, or the first 30 minutes on Monday before the inbox): list every commitment (calendar, unwritten promises, recurring duties) down the left of a page and cross them all out ("commitment bankruptcy"). Then imagine $1,000 where each commitment costs $100 to buy back, write what you rebuy on the right, and circle the ones you bought back only because you "should"
- **Reverse pilot:** for one week remove or shrink a reluctant commitment (skip a low-value meeting, shorten a report), then ask whether anyone noticed and whether your life got measurably worse; if neither, keep it cut
- **One essential priority a week** (your highest point of contribution): the right thing is where Aptitude, what is Essential to others, and what you are Interested in overlap; the right reason is that it matters to you rather than pressure or pleasing; the right time is the tiebreaker
- **90% rule:** score the weekly priority as 100 and rate each new request against it; anything under 90 becomes 0, so options scoring 70-89 stop tugging at you. It is a numeric version of Derek Sivers' "if it's not a hell yeah, it's a no"
- **"No, but":** default to no on big asks but offer a smaller valuable help (decline the committee seat, offer to draft its charter), trading short-term popularity for long-term respect
